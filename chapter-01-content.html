Kaelen Voss — Salvage Worker

Works the hulls of the Junkyard alone, pricing copper against fuel. Twenty-four, wrist-deep in debt, and the proud owner of exactly one escape pod that has escaped once. When the seam fights, he takes the shoulder.

The cutter bit, bucked, and dragged Kaelen Voss shoulder-first across the hull.

He could have caught himself. The tether had enough give, and his left hand was free. But the salvage blade was already chewing through the fused-armor seam at an angle that would take another pass to fix, and another pass meant another fuel cell, and fuel cells were twelve credits he didn’t have. So he let the hull do what it did best—throw him—and counted on his shoulder being cheaper than the blade wandering.

It was. The harness dug under his ribs, his right shoulder struck and slid, and the blade ground back into the seam. Good. Shoulder was a renewable resource. Cutter passes were not.

He locked his elbow and flattened himself against the plating until the pull stopped. The blade could fight him. The hull did not get to throw him.

Sunlight spilled over the wreck in hard gold, flashing from the cutter’s casing and turning every nick in his visor into a white scratch. Below him—if directions still meant anything—the Junkyard turned in its slow congregation: freighters peeled to their ribs, engine bells wider than houses, habitat drums opened to vacuum and stuffed with smaller dead machines. Loose cables drifted between them. Insulation crossed the light in silver flashes and vanished black.

Bright. Cold. He’d been looking at this view for three years and still caught himself admiring it sometimes—a half-breath between one pass and the next, nothing more. The station’s own kids called it the Boneyard. The riggers who worked it called it home. Same difference.

The Junkyard Below

The dead freighter had once hauled seed vats before its registry vanished and its name burned away. Rows of heat blisters still marked the outer plating where growing lamps had run behind the hull, each raised welt the width of his hand. Kaelen had followed them all morning. Intact copper buswork paid by length. Armor paid by mass and cost twice as much fuel to move. Copper paid. Armor burned fuel. Today, copper won.

He needed both kinds of pay. Before the recycler membrane failed. Copper first. Armor only if one pass opened it. He could afford stubborn machinery or bad arithmetic, not both.

Below him, the Halo’s patched ring carried its small life: warm kitchens behind cold bulkheads, sleeping cubbies that smelled of damp cloth, floors that carried every dropped tool into the rooms below. The recycler membrane in his pod had begun leaving a sweet taste at the back of his throat, thickest after sleep—the fungal membrane needed replacing before it made the choice for him. Eleven weeks of berth fees on top of that. Not counting them had achieved nothing.

One seam, four debts. Membrane. Cell. Food. Fees.

The seam did not open cleanly.

A tremor rolled along the hull—slow, heavy, strong enough to make his teeth touch. Not his tool. The wreck shifted beneath him.

The Flicker on the Hull

A thin crust of frozen sealant broke loose near his visor. The flakes rose from the plating and hung there. Two gloved hands appeared in the corner of his visor, ghost-pale and a fraction late. Kaelen’s jaw tightened. Not now. He held still until the false fingers settled over his real hands and the doubled edge of the hull became one solid line again.

Boundary people knew the rule: when sight doubled, stop the blade, touch something bolted down, and wait for the double image to settle. Children learned it before they learned station letters. Adults followed it when the work allowed.

The tremor came again. This one rolled along the hull hard enough to rattle the cutter against its mount.

Kaelen lifted his head. Far off, the station’s main spindle flashed between drifting mountains of scrap. Its long central shaft caught the sun in broken strips where generations of repair plating overlapped. Docking spars radiated from it in six bright rows. The spindle was the station’s spine; its movement came through walls, cups, bedframes, and the soles of sleeping children before anyone heard an alarm.

One row was moving.

The vibration in his boots cut out. Not reduced—gone, like a throat closing mid-word.

Spar Three had torn loose at its root. Its sunlit end swung outward, carrying docked hulks and pressurized work pods with it. Connections burst in white jets. One jet struck a turning cargo shell and sent it rolling. A gantry folded at the middle, its trusses closing like fingers before the whole structure shed glittering fragments. People he half-knew worked those spars. Someone’s wall had just folded. He could not tell whose.

Spar Three Tears Loose

Kaelen had followed enough of those seams to know the Junkyard’s story without being told. Six docking spars from three shipyards, their truss patterns meeting the spindle through adapter collars layered with welds and inspection paint. The Halo was a ring of workshops, kitchens, and pressure shelters built from whatever each decade could seal — old white ceramic, black ship armor, yellow cargo plating, bare metal darkened around fresh welds. Every hatch took a different wrench. Every corridor bent where a wall had been useful. If the spar hit it, one fuel cell would not get him there.

He trusted it because he maintained it himself. He distrusted it for the same reason.

His suit radio filled with overlapping voices.

"—lost anchor—"

"Pressure drop on Blue Nine!"

"Clear the spindle. Clear everything."

A work crew called roll in the gaps between warnings. Someone demanded the location of two children from the sealant shop. Someone else answered that they were already in Blue Nine and had carried the shop’s old woman with them. A rigger shouted for everyone to cut loose mass, then swore when three crews asked which mass he meant. Names answered, one less missing. Silence put them back. The spars workers kept reporting pressures because numbers were easier to hold than the thought of those walls opening.

Then the station alarm reached Kaelen through the hull: three blunt impacts, repeated by the metal under his gloves. The blows traveled through his forearms and into his chest.

Kaelen released the jammed cutter head and kicked toward his escape pod.

The head stayed buried in the armor. Its toothed edge remained wedged in a black groove, one side still smoking faintly where the last of the heat bled away. He left half a day’s work, his best blade, and a claim marker carrying his name. Junkyard salvage law was plain: if you abandoned a marked cut during a station alarm, the cut remained yours until the all-clear. Junkyard practice was plainer: after a spindle failure, anything not attached to your body belonged to whoever reached it alive.

Calling it an escape pod was generous. It had escaped once. Enough for one hull. Its original white shell had yellowed beneath patches of gray cargo plate. One side was ribbed by uneven welds, and the hatch sat lower at one corner than the other. Kaelen had welded tool racks across two couches, patched the aft shell with cargo plating, and filled the third couch well with ration bricks. Thirty-two days of food remained if he kept eating half portions. The maneuvering cluster had one charged fuel cell.

The Pod

The pod was also the only sealed thing he owned.

There were people in Halo Two who owned less. Three families slept behind the ceramic shop’s kiln because the kiln wall held heat after station night. The sealant workers kept a common pot on sixth shift and added water for anyone whose account had closed. When a pressure siren sounded, nobody asked whose child had arrived at which shelter. Space was made. Names came later.

Kaelen had eaten from that pot twice. He had repaired its lid and called the balance settled. The sealant workers had ignored him and filled his bowl again the next week.

He caught the hatch rail with both hands. His body swung past the opening, tether and tool belt pulling in different directions, and he brought one boot down against the sill to stop himself. He hauled himself inside.

The door fought him over a warped lower hinge. Kaelen put both hands on the inner grip and pulled until the tendons stood out along his wrists. The seal touched at the top and left a dark gap below. He drove his heel into the lower corner once, twice, then a third time. The metal jumped into place. The dogs engaged with a staggered series of clunks.

He stripped off his helmet. Sweat cooled at once along his hairline. His face felt pinched from the suit pressure, and the muscles at the back of his neck trembled from the cutter’s last kick. He slapped the pod awake.

Amber panels trembled into life. Their light picked out every scrape and grease print inside the cramped cabin. Netting sagged over the ration bricks. Tools crowded the racks above the couches, their handles wrapped in mismatched tape. A dark stain marked the deck beneath the recycler housing where condensation had leaked and dried.

Then every panel screamed red.

The warning tone sawed through the cabin, too sharp to ignore. Three bands of light struck across the console in sequence, each one brighter than the last, and the words punched into the viewport as if the pod meant to hammer them through Kaelen's skull.

SPINDLE EVENT
CASCADE FAILURE PROBABILITY: 71%
LOCAL IMPACT WINDOW: 00:01:46

The deck vibrated in time with the alarm tone—not a hum, a pulse, each beat landing in the soles of his boots and climbing his spine. The red light painted his hands on the console, the cracks in the viewport, the grease smears on his sleeves. Everything the light touched looked like it was bleeding.

One minute forty-six seconds. The number sat in the center of the display, bright and exact, counting down to the moment the spindle tore loose and swept through the keel like a blade through wet paper. His pod was parked in the keel.

The timer pulsed. Each flash stripped the cabin to red, then back to amber. Kaelen's heartbeat had found the alarm's rhythm and locked to it.

"Optimist," Kaelen said, and reached for the release controls.

The port clamp answered with a solid vibration under the deck. The starboard clamp did not. Its status glyph held green, but Kaelen knew the difference between a sensor and a machine. Last month he had packed the clamp’s worn jaw with copper mesh to keep it seated. The mesh could hold against a normal release. A wreck twisting under load was not normal.

He hooked one boot under the couch frame and struck the manual release twice with the heel of his hand.

The stiff switch bruised his palm.

*Ow.*

His mouth pulled tight. He shook the hand once and hit the switch again.

Nothing.

The impact window reached one minute thirty-eight.

A concussion ran through the wreck.

The pod’s walls flexed inward with a hollow boom. The cutter, still clipped outside, struck the hatch. Ration bricks jumped in their netting and settled crooked. A wrench tore loose from its magnetic strip, hit a couch back, and spun across the cabin.

Kaelen caught the control frame with both hands as his pod’s docking clamps gave a metallic groan. The sound came through the shell as much as the air, low enough to vibrate his fingertips.

Something hit the forward viewport.

The glass starred. White fracture lines jumped from the point of impact, sharp against the dark beyond.

Kaelen flinched back, forearm rising across his face.

It was only a bolt, thick as his wrist.

Beyond it came the plate.

A slab of hull armor spun across the gap, its cut edges blazing in the sun. It filled the viewport faster than his eyes could adjust to its size. Charred paint, torn reinforcement ribs, and frozen beads of coolant flashed across its face with each rotation.

Red light from the proximity warning painted his chest.

Impact in less than a second.

His hand moved toward the thruster trigger.

*Too slow.*

His reaching hand stopped short.

The world changed.

The warning tone deepened until each pulse became a separate pressure against his eardrums. The cabin seemed to tighten around him. Dust hung above the console: pale grains of insulation, a black shaving from a cable sheath, a bright crumb of metal. The hull plate’s rotation opened into distinct degrees: bright face, dark edge, bright face.

Kaelen felt his own heartbeat pushing through him, heavy and unwilling. His throat worked against a breath that had stopped halfway in.

His heartbeat reached his ears half a beat after it struck his ribs.

The school posters called that a Flicker. Check both clocks. Sit down. Do not operate cutting equipment or pressure doors. Report repeated events to the nearest Thread-Watcher station.

Kaelen’s mechanical clock had fallen off the wall six months ago. The bare mount still showed above the tool rack, two screws and a cleaner rectangle in the grime. The station-linked display now showed five different impact times, changing too quickly to read.

Lines covered the viewport.

They were finer than scratches and too dark to be reflections. Each ran from the tumbling plate into a different place: the pod’s nose, the docking spar, the empty black above his shoulder. Some lay straight. Others bent around the plate’s turning edge. Measurements crowded his mind without numbers—distance, arrival, consequence. They pressed against one another until his eyes watered.

The Armor Slab and the First Thread

He understood none of it and knew exactly which outcomes killed him.

One line ended where the plate cut the pod in half. One left the pod intact but tore both clamps from the wreck, sending him spinning with no stable firing angle. A third passed close to the starboard flank and continued into open dark.

One line passed beneath the pod.

*That one.*

Kaelen seized it.

The line had no surface, yet it drew tight against his grip like a thread joining the pod to one possible place. It was one nearby outcome made sharp enough to feel and cold enough to numb the hand that touched it. Kaelen knew only that the thread beneath him did not end in impact.

His fingers clamped around the thruster control. Cold drove through his palm, though he touched only worn polymer warmed by the cabin. The chill sank beneath the skin so quickly that his knuckles locked. He pulled sideways.

The pod fired.

Time struck him all at once. The maneuvering jet cracked against the wreck, slamming Kaelen into the couch. The harness caught across his chest and hips. His head snapped back against the padding. The armor plate crossed the viewport close enough to erase the sun, leaving the cabin dark for one hard beat.

Its edge clipped the external cutter and took it away.

The cabin lights steadied.

Kaelen lay against the harness, eyes squeezed shut, tasting blood where his teeth had cut his tongue. His breath came in through his nose and left between clenched teeth. When he opened his eyes, the skin around them felt tight.

The fuel cell whined beneath the deck. The starboard clamp had released after all, but its jaw hung open on the external view, copper packing trailing from it in a bright little cloud. His pod had moved sideways without completing the rotation the thruster should have imposed. The plate had passed around a space he had not known could exist.

Kaelen looked at his right hand.

It was still wrapped around the control. His palm had gone white. The nails carried a faint blue cast, and his fingers shook when he forced them open. Nothing in the pod could make flesh that cold without freezing the metal first.

On the salvage channel, someone whispered, "Voss."

A second voice said, "I saw that."

The first worker had watched the plate close over the pod and had already pictured the opened cabin, its tools and ration bricks turning among the scrap. Now the pod remained whole where his eyes said it should not be. Anything ordinary would do. A thruster fault. A bad range trace. Anything that would leave the world familiar. His gloved hand stayed locked around the rail until his wrist hurt.

Kaelen flexed his right hand. Pain prickled along each finger. A black lattice lingered across his vision, branching from every moving object, then faded.

"What did you fire at?" the first voice demanded.

"The plate."

"You moved before it turned."

Kaelen wiped his mouth with his sleeve. A red streak marked the fabric. "No, I didn’t."

"You moved while my clock dropped eight-tenths."

Another worker broke in. "My clock didn’t drop."

"Your clock loses a minute every shift."

"It loses it regularly."

The second worker’s laugh did not come. Regularly was safe. If the old clock was wrong in the usual way, the station still made sense. If it was wrong in a new way, neither of them wanted to say what they had witnessed.

"Both of you clear the channel," Halo control said.

The station alarm battered through the hull again.

Kaelen checked the fuel readout. The short burn had taken four percent. The docking clamps showed green, though the wreck around them was beginning to rotate. The stars beyond the viewport had started a slow slide, interrupted each time a drifting piece of salvage crossed them.

Four percent for less than a pod-length. Bad price.

It had also taken his cutter.

Nine months. Gone.

The lost tool represented nine months of small repairs, skipped hot meals, and a favor owed to a forge mechanic who kept exact accounts. The old irritation rose first. It tightened his mouth and brought heat back into his face. Familiar. Useful. He put it aside with the rest of the arithmetic.

He released the harness and planted both feet on the deck. His knees nearly folded. He held the couch frame until the weakness passed.

One breath. Then another. His fingertips tingled painfully as warmth returned.

He rolled his right shoulder, pressed his palm to the deck, and counted the bolts under his fingers. Twelve along the couch rail. Four around the pressure access. One missing by the ration bin. The exercise was older than the school posters. A real place had edges. A real place stayed where you touched it.

The deck felt cold, ridged, and solid beneath his palm.

The pod stayed.

A structural display unfolded across the console. Its light painted Kaelen’s face red and amber. Spar Three’s departure had twisted the spindle, pulling neighboring docks out of alignment. Kaelen’s section—Maintenance Halo Two—remained attached, but a pressure pulse was racing through the connected service trunks. The diagram painted compartments red in sequence.

His compartment stood at the end.

So did Blue Nine.

Kaelen had been to Blue Nine a dozen times⁆ it was the halo’s largest pressure refuge, a converted coolant depot where the broad floor still held blue stain around the old drains. Tool cabinets stood against one curved wall; folded bedding and cooking tins filled the gaps beneath them. The inner chamber’s walls carried handprints in six colors, one set for every seal replacement that had held through an alarm.

Now bodies crowded beneath those handprints. Workers leaned shoulder-first into the inner hatches while families drew their knees close to make room. The air smelled of machine oil, old coolant, and the hot cloth of blankets pulled down too quickly.

The pod could survive vacuum. The families in Blue Nine could not.

Kaelen leaned close to the console. The cold in his hand was fading, but his fingertips still dragged against the controls.

"Blue Nine, shut your inner hatches," Kaelen said.

"We’re trying," someone answered. "Frame’s buckled — Can’t get the inner seals to bite."

The speaker had both boots planted against a tool cabinet and one shoulder under the warped brace. The fourth hatch rode above its track, leaving a black line where the seal should have pressed flat. Kaelen could hear it in her voice—she was the only thing between that gap and the families behind her.

She drove her shoulder harder into the brace. Metal bit through her work jacket.

A second voice came through behind the first, older and steadier. "Third is seated. Fourth is riding high."

Metal hammered in the background. Breath hissed close to someone’s transmitter.

A child was counting. The voice was small, clear, and too fast.

Not seconds. Door teeth.

Counting the Teeth

Counting made the teeth a task. One, then the next. The child kept her eyes on the teeth and away from the gap. If the child reached the last one and began again, the adults might still be working and the room might still have air. Kaelen heard her start again through the hammering. Fine. She had door teeth. He had pressure.

The pressure pulse advanced.

Kaelen zoomed the maintenance plan. Shafts webbed the halo, most dead-ended by old modifications. Some lines ended at blank walls; others crossed passages that had been removed years ago. One narrow trunk ran inward and down toward an abandoned smelter intake. The route was open, but the pulse would pass its junction instead of turning into it.

The plan was twelve revisions out of date. Kaelen knew because he had crawled M-Seventeen during a heat leak and found a patched branch that did not exist on any public layer. Halo workers kept private maps on grease cloth and traded them with tools.

He pulled his own from a locker. The folded cloth was stiff with old lubricant and warm from lying against the pod wall. He spread it over the console, pinning two curling corners with a spanner and a ration brick. Grease marks crossed hand-drawn shafts in brown and black. Kaelen flattened the last crease with his wrist and matched stains to the red station diagram.

"Control, Blue Eleven still tied into the old wash trunk?" he asked.

"No. Ceramic shop sealed it last winter."

"Smelter furnace?"

"Open to vacuum. Mouth plate went for scrap."

A way out, then, if the pulse could be made to take it.

Speed, angle, force. The pulse owned the first. M-Seventeen offered the second. The dead actuator could supply the third, if Halo control woke it.

Kaelen’s eyes moved between the grease-cloth map and the advancing red compartments. His lips parted with each silent count.

Shut every hatch? Too many frames had shifted. Blow the trunk at its root? That vented the machine shops and pulled Blue Nine’s crooked fourth seal outward when it needed to seat.

Every other move spent air or people. M-Seventeen spent pressure already in motion.

The dark lines returned.

Kaelen’s shoulders locked. His right hand curled against the console before he made it open again.

Fine. Show him.

The lines did not cross his sight this time. They lay inside the structure, tension drawn through metal and air. The sensation ran beneath his boots and up his legs until the pod’s deck felt like the station’s skin. The approaching wave pressed futures ahead of itself. In one, it struck Blue Nine. In another, a service hatch failed first. In a third, the smelter trunk took the force and vented it into a furnace chamber open to space.

More futures pressed at the edges: bolts lifting, collars splitting, the actuator wheel turning too late. Each seemed near enough to touch and gone before he named it. His eyes flicked across empty sections of the cabin, following things no one else could see. He did not know whether he was seeing what would happen or measuring what might.

Didn’t matter yet.

Blue Nine had twenty-three seconds.

That future lay close, but not close enough.

Kaelen braced his knees beneath the console. The metal edge dug through his trousers. He wrapped one hand around the frame until his grip stopped shaking.

"Halo control, can you cycle Shaft M-Seventeen?"

"M-Seventeen is manual."

"Power the actuator."

"It won’t change the flow angle."

"It won’t if it isn’t powered."

A pause. "Actuator live."

The control voice did not sound convinced. Busy was better.

"Worker at M-Seventeen," Kaelen said, "clear the wheel."

"I’m on the wheel."

The worker’s gloves were slick with old grease. The wheel kicked beneath them each time pressure struck the trunk, and his forearms had begun to burn. Letting go felt like abandoning Blue Nine. He knew people behind that door. Everyone in the Halo knew people behind every door.

"Then let go."

"If I let go, it spins back."

"If you keep hold, it takes your wrists."

The worker swore, but a scrape came through the channel as boots pushed away. Kaelen had better be right. His hands remained curved in front of him after he released the wheel, ready to seize it again if Kaelen was wrong.

Kaelen set his left hand on the manual map. The grease cloth slid beneath his damp palm. With his right, he traced the trunk from the pressure front to the open furnace. Existing pressure. Existing opening. Existing actuator. He needed no new force, only one change: the direction the wave took at the junction.

The Second Ability

One variable.

His tongue pressed against the cut inside his mouth. The taste of blood sharpened.

Kaelen closed his hand around the future in which the wave turned.

The cold went deeper. It entered his wrist, climbed the bones of his arm, and hooked behind his ribs. His back arched. Breath left him in one short sound.

For an instant he could feel the maintenance trunk’s dimensions: every dent, welded collar, grease-stiff bearing, and strip of sealant lifting from the walls. The roughness of the shaft seemed to scrape along the inside of his arm. The actuator’s weight sat in his palm.

He pulled.

Across the halo, loose dust tilted toward Shaft M-Seventeen before the air moved.

Voices from Blue Nine described the dust rising from the floor and sliding sideways over boots before the air moved. Someone had gone quiet. The worker at the fourth hatch said her forearms were lifting.

Kaelen’s jaw tightened. They were still in there.

Three workers on the emergency channel began speaking at once. Good. They were shouting about the change, not the impact.

Kaelen’s eyes widened. Every muscle in his right forearm stood tight beneath the skin.

She kept them there.

Kaelen felt the wave turn.

For one stretched instant, the pressure belonged to both routes. His teeth ached with it. The red line on the display forked toward Blue Nine and the smelter at once. The cabin air seemed to push against one side of his face, then the other.

A wrench trembled across the deck until it struck the ration bin. On the structural display, the advancing red line bent sharply away from Blue Nine.

A woman said, "Fourth is green. All inner seals green."

Halo control repeated it across the emergency band. The words moved from workshop to workshop. People answered with the old pressure-shelter knock: four taps on metal, a pause, then one more for the person listening outside.

The knock reached Kaelen through the pod’s hull. It came from more than one direction, muted by layers of plating, each set arriving a little after the last.

His legs folded. His hip struck the couch frame, and he sat hard on the deck between the couches. He stayed there with one shoulder pressed against the ration bin, breathing through his nose while the cabin appeared to tilt around him.

Later.

Outside, fragments drifted through clean sunlight. Some moved on ordinary courses. Others left pale doubles on the pod’s damaged glass, occupying two positions until Kaelen blinked. Each blink tugged at the ache behind his eye.

Of course. Another beacon.

Keel crews often bought old suits with the company channel stripped out. No feed meant no remote brace, no blood-oxygen reading, and no one telling her when to panic.

Tamsin saw the station in pieces as she turned: Halo lights, black space, the bright flank of a wreck, then black again. Her left arm moved inside the suit with a deep loose pain that sharpened each time her body crossed the sun. The oxygen timer sat near the edge of her visor. She had stopped watching it. Let the timer count alone. The number fell whether she looked or not.

Tamsin Vale — Keel Miner

Drifts the station’s keel shoots where no registry goes and no map is public. Sturdy as the rock she cuts, with the sealant-smudged confidence of someone who has hidden an exit plan. Not much in the Junkyard surprises her.

Kaelen looked at the fuel gauge. Ninety-six percent. The green bar looked full until he noticed the thin gap at its end. Enough to release, reach her, and perhaps correct back toward the station. Not enough if the spindle’s rotation worsened.

That fuel was next month. Spend it and the pod stayed where a tug left it. His claim, membrane, berth account, and replacement cell went with it.

Two minutes out. Five back. Six minutes of oxygen. Caution would kill her.

Nothing else mattered.

"Tamsin, can you stop your spin?"

"My left shoulder’s out."

"Clamp your right hand to your chest."

"Why?"

"So I can catch you without tearing it off."

"You planning to come out here?"

Through each turn, Tamsin searched for the pod that had answered. When she found it, the patched shell looked too small, too old, and too firmly clamped to the wreck. Hope arrived anyway.

The pod could not miss.

The thought tightened her good hand against her chest. Hope was unwelcome because it gave the timer something new to take from her.

Kaelen unlocked the pod’s clamps.

The port status changed. The starboard mechanism gave no response. Its copper packing was gone, leaving the jaw slightly open on the exterior feed.

Kaelen crouched and reached under the console. His shoulder pressed against a nest of warm cables. He found the emergency release cable by touch, pulled its stiff length free, and wound it twice around his left forearm. The braided metal bit through his sleeve.

Halo control cut in. "Voss, negative. Debris field is accelerating."

"The next craft is twelve minutes out."

"We know."

"She has six."

"We know that too."

There was no lecture in the control voice, only the strain of too many correct facts occupying the same moment. The operator had watched three rescue traces fail to become useful. Kaelen’s pod was the wrong craft with the wrong fuel and the only arrival inside Tamsin’s air.

Kaelen planted both heels against the deck and pulled the cable. His body tightened from shoulders to calves. It tore skin through his sleeve and moved three centimeters.

The clamps released with two sharp knocks.

The pod drifted free. Kaelen’s stomach lifted as the wreck receded beyond the viewport.

His cut line, claim marker, and buried cutter shrank against the freighter’s gold hull. The claim marker remained visible for a moment as a small blue light beside the black seam. For a moment the ordinary loss hurt more than the new pain behind his eye. The cut line and cutter had prices he knew. They would appear on account screens, and enough shifts could replace them.

"Voss," Tamsin said, "that pod has one working jet."

"It has three."

"I can see it."

"Then you know which side to stay on."

"That’s the side currently facing me."

"I’ll improve it."

Tamsin watched the pod’s bad angle repeat with every turn. The joke helped. Good. He could keep arguing. Someone who could waste breath arguing with her had not yet decided she was dead.

He fired the aft cluster.

The couch kicked against his back. Loose tools strained against their straps, and the ration net drew tight over the bricks. The remaining fuel cell dropped under load, its percentage falling faster than distance closed.

The Rescue Run Through the Debris Field

Debris crossed the route: yellowed insulation folded around a dark core, cable turning end over end, a yellow pressure tank, a fan of glass that appeared only when its edges caught the sun. Each shard turned bright and clear, then disappeared against the stars.

The clean route curved too wide. The direct route crossed the glass fan and passed close enough to the pressure tank to read its inspection stamps.

Kaelen chose direct.

Ugly still worked. It usually did.

He rotated the pod so its patched flank took the smallest shards. The thruster vibration shivered through his spine. Glass whispered across the hull, a dry hiss that moved from nose to stern. A splinter drove into the outer cargo plate and stayed there, shining over the camera feed.

The yellow tank rolled nearer. Its paint had been scorched brown on one side and rubbed to bare metal around the middle. Its inspection stamp was eight years old. A white jet hissed from its neck, changing its spin every time the leak faced sunward. Kaelen could cross before it on a full burn, pass behind it on a slow one, or kill thrust and let the pod’s current line carry him under.

Full burn left too little for the return. Slow meant Tamsin lost another minute. Killing thrust put the tank between the rescue clamp and the beacon.

"Pressure tank crossing," he said.

"I saw it three turns ago," Tamsin replied.

"Any advice?"

"Don’t hit it."

"Useful. Stay available."

The tank passed so close that chipped yellow paint and a dent around one valve filled the viewport. Its leaking gas whitened the cracked glass and turned the stars into a pale blur. The pod shuddered in the plume. Kaelen’s shoulders jerked against the harness. He pulsed the lateral jet, once, then let orbital motion do the rest.

The plume reached Tamsin as a thin white veil. For one turn, it hid the pod from her. She counted her breath, waited for the patched nose to return, and found that she had clenched her good hand against her chest exactly as Kaelen had ordered.

Fuel dropped to sixty-two percent.

A strip of torn insulation spread across the route. It looked soft, layers of white batting opening and closing as it tumbled. Its wire mesh was not. Kaelen angled the pod’s old hatch toward it, heard the mesh scrape in a long metallic drag, and watched three exterior tool hooks disappear.

Halo control said, "Your return line is closing."

"I know."

"Spindle rotation has increased by one point four degrees."

"I know."

"Do you know anything encouraging?"

Kaelen watched Tamsin’s silver arm turn against the black. At each rotation her visor caught the sun. His lips compressed as he counted the intervals.

"Ask me when she’s inside."

Tamsin heard the answer on the outward half of a turn. The station had begun to slide away beneath her, its lights gathering closer together with distance. She held on to the word inside. It was a place, not a promise, and that made it easier to trust.

Kaelen rolled his shoulders and loosened his right hand. The fingers resisted, stiff from the cold. Seeing the near paths had left pressure in his mind. He still knew the discarded routes after looking away, and each demanded a different burn. They sat behind his eyes like remembered movements.

He could not afford to reach for the dark lines blindly.

The pod, the beacon, the rescue clamp: three moving things. The debris supplied the board.

The black lattice unfolded again.

Kaelen sucked a breath between his teeth. Pain cinched behind his right eye. Cold gathered in the center of his palm, deeper than skin. The muscles along his jaw stood out as he kept his gaze on Tamsin.

One clean path. Enough.

He did not slow time. He could not find the inward grip of it. Instead he saw the path his pod would take, then the paths that nearly belonged to it. They lay across one another, dark and precise, each ending at a slightly different angle.

He nudged between them.

To Tamsin, watching through a cracked visor, the pod seemed to arrive in three places and discard two.

The three images lay across one another: three patched noses, three cracked viewports, three ways to die against the same hull. Tamsin’s breath caught. She wanted to blame the visor, the oxygen, the spin. Then two pods thinned and the remaining one kept coming.

Three Arrivals

The sight offered a clean arrival and two survivable failures. In one, the pod overshot but its wake pushed Tamsin toward the wreck. In another, her boot caught the clamp and broke her leg. The clean arrival existed only if her ring crossed the clamp’s field at the end of her next rotation.

Seeing was not enough to make it happen.

"Tamsin, on my mark, straighten your right leg."

"My left shoulder is out, not my hearing."

"Good. Mark."

She kicked.

Tamsin chose the voice and the solid pod between the other two. Her right leg snapped straight. The movement pulled her torso out of line, and her injured arm shifted inside the suit. Her face tightened behind the scratched visor. White pain pressed her lips back from her teeth. The motion changed her spin less than Kaelen hoped and more than his fuel allowed.

He matched her spin badly.

The pod rolled alongside her with its damaged flank low. Tamsin’s boots struck the hull. The impact folded her at the hips, and her body rolled toward the live thruster.

Heat brightened against one side of her suit. The nozzle opened across her visor, close enough for her to see soot around its rim.

He’d told her which side.

She found it anyway.

Kaelen’s stomach dropped. His hand slammed over the control before his brain finished the thought.

He cut the thruster.

The cabin straps loosened around him as acceleration vanished. Tamsin’s helmet missed the hot nozzle by the width of his hand, but her emergency ring swept past the rescue clamp.

The pod rolled inside three metres. Tamsin’s spin carried the ring away again.

The clamp fell behind her shoulder. She saw it once, small and useless, and knew she had no good hand free to reach it.

*No.*

Her fingers clawed at empty space. The pod had come. She had done what she was told.

Kaelen caught another thread—the future position of her emergency tether ring—and pulled it toward the pod’s rescue clamp.

The cold drove through the same path in his hand. His fingers curled as if closing around a metal loop. This time he knew the shape he wanted: not Tamsin, not the pod, not even the clamp.

One ring. One position. One click.

The ring met the clamp with a clean click.

The sound reached Tamsin through her suit and tether as a small hard tap. For an instant she did not understand it. Then the line drew tight at her waist, and relief struck so sharply that it felt like another injury.

Tamsin jerked to a stop against the hull. Her body folded around the tether point, then rebounded. Her good hand slapped flat against the plating.

Her suit alarm began shrilling over the channel. Kaelen released the thread at once. His fingers sprang open. The black lines thinned but did not vanish.

Fuel remaining: nine percent.

The lattice remained when he blinked, offering routes he no longer had fuel to take.

"Are you attached?" Kaelen asked.

Tamsin’s breathing scraped over the channel. "I’ve developed a close relationship with your hull."

"Keep it professional."

"Open the hatch."

Her glove stayed spread against the pod’s plating. The metal’s vibration came through her palm, faint but steady.

This was ground.

After open space, even the pod’s rough welds felt like ground.

The rescue reel motor had not worked in four years. Its housing occupied a dented box beneath the hatch, the service cover held shut by two different screws and a strip of sealant.

Kaelen locked his boots under the couch rail and pulled the line by hand. The rough cable dragged through his gloves. Each turn of the reel sent Tamsin’s mass into the pod’s flank with a dull impact. The magnetic skin struggled to keep them aligned with the wreck. Its coils hummed beneath the deck, rising in pitch after every strike.

A shard of gantry passed under them. Its broken truss scraped along the pod’s lower surface. The lower antenna snapped off without ceremony, and its status light went dark.

At the hatch, Tamsin caught the rail with her right hand exactly as told. Her glove clamped around it hard enough to whiten the knuckle plates. Her left arm floated wrong inside the suit, elbow low and shoulder lifted against the collar. The hatch frame scraped her hip as the pod rolled.

All she had to do was keep the good hand closed.

She held the rail and thought of nothing else.

Kaelen braced his boots beside the warped door. His back pressed against the opposite wall. He guided the rescue line so the ring would not torque her injured shoulder.

Reeled In by Hand

Kaelen reeled her to the hatch, sealed it, and burned the last charge correcting toward Halo Two. The maneuvering cluster vibrated unevenly, one jet kicking harder than the others. The acceleration pressed him back into the couch—not the hard crack of the first burn, just a tired shove that said the fuel cell was done trying. The indicator reached zero before their velocity matched.

They struck a maintenance bumper sideways.

The pod wall boomed. The impact threw Kaelen and Tamsin against the ration net. Two bricks broke loose. Tamsin caught one against her chest with her good arm. Kaelen caught the other with his forehead.

Bright pain flashed across his vision. His head snapped back, and he stayed still for a moment with his eyes shut.

Fair.

He lifted one hand to the rising bruise.

Tamsin’s boots tangled in the netting. Her bad shoulder struck padding instead of metal, but the shock still ran white down her arm. She curled around the ration brick and waited for the pod to split.

The pod had better not split.

The air remained warm against her face.

The pod rebounded once, rolled a fraction, and stayed there, held by its magnetic skin. The coils hummed under strain. Outside the viewport, the same tiled section of Halo Two stopped moving.

Halo control’s voice came through the restored station link. "Pod Twelve, magnetic contact confirmed. Do not attempt release."

Kaelen rubbed the rising mark above his eyebrow. "Wasn’t planning to."

"Medical team is pinned behind Blue Seven. Estimate nine minutes."

Tamsin looked at her oxygen display. Her broad chest still rose too fast beneath the suit, but her breathing had begun to settle. The timer no longer owned the room. "I can wait nine."

"You said your left shoulder was out."

"It remains out. We’re becoming acquainted."

Kaelen found the pod’s inflatable splint and a roll of pressure cloth. The medical roll had once been white. Age and handling had turned its outer wrapper gray, though the seal still broke with a dry snap. The splint’s printed instructions had worn away. Shoulders had not changed.

He cut Tamsin’s suit sleeve loose at the seal. The fabric resisted the blade, then parted. Her shoulder sat lower than it should, and the muscles along her neck held rigid. Sweat gleamed at her temples inside the opened helmet ring. Kaelen checked that her fingers were warm and pressed each nail until color returned. He inflated the brace until she told him to stop.

She told him to stop three increments before he did.

Tamsin’s face went still. Her nostrils flared, and the fingers of her good hand crushed the ration brick against her chest.

Not yet. Not while the shoulder was untouched.

She had endured the loose joint by refusing to imagine anyone touching it. Now every small swell of the brace gave the pain a new edge.

"Miner," Kaelen said, "I need that hand later."

"Salvager, I need the shoulder now."

He backed the pressure off one increment. The brace softened around the joint. Tamsin’s jaw unlocked a fraction.

She accepted the compromise by not kicking him.

For several seconds neither spoke.

The cabin smelled of hot insulation, suit metal, and the sharp green balm from the medical roll. A trace of blood remained in Kaelen’s mouth. The recycler pushed warm air through the pod in uneven breaths, carrying the sweet aftertaste of its failing membrane.

Tamsin breathed that bad air and found it excellent.

It was the best air she had ever tasted.

The pod was cramped, overheated, and crowded with tools that would become knives in a hard turn. It was sealed. She could feel the deck through her boots and another person moving through the frame.

Outside the cracked viewport, the Maintenance Halo rotated past: patchwork walls, sun mirrors, laundry drums, and the bright ceramic tiles children had fixed around old impact scars. At Blue Nine, four hatch lights shone green.

Then Tamsin set her good hand against the deck and pushed herself upright.

Her arm trembled once under her weight. She shifted until her back rested against the couch frame. Kaelen took in the rest without meaning to: broad-faced, close-cropped, silver sealant smeared across one cheek and a red pressure mark around her brow from the helmet liner. Fine lines at the corners of her eyes deepened when she studied something. Her left arm hung in an inflated splint, held close against her torso.

Tamsin

"You’re Kaelen Voss," she said.

"That’s on the hatch."

"You bent a pressure wave."

"The shaft did."

"And you pulled my tether from six meters away."

"The clamp has a strong field."

Tamsin studied him, breathing evenly now. Her eyes moved from his pale right hand to the blood drying at the corner of his mouth. "You’re bad at lying."

She had seen the pod divide and watched two versions vanish. She had felt her tether ring move where her body had not. The easy choice was to blame low oxygen. Kaelen’s white palm made that choice harder.

"I work alone. It doesn’t come up often."

She looked past him at the net of ration bricks, the single patched pressure suit, and the two couches buried under tools. One couch carried coils of cable sorted by thickness. The other held a cutter casing, three sealed jars of fasteners, and a folded thermal sheet stiff with old repairs. Nothing in the pod suggested a second person had ever been expected.

"That’s expensive work alone," she said.

"Other people charge."

"They also carry you when the cutter bucks."

"The tether did."

"The tether isn’t going to argue with you."

"It needs a new buckle. It has standing."

Tamsin’s mouth moved at one corner. The expression pulled at the silver sealant on her cheek. She turned her wrist panel toward him. The screen’s blue light washed across her fingers.

Her mining shift had begun six hours earlier. Four cargo claims were listed under her crew license, all in the keel interior. The crew roster beneath them was blank.

Kaelen’s gaze settled on the empty roster. "You were working alone too."

"Temporary disagreement."

"With the crew?"

"With employment."

The pod chimed as station systems reconnected. Tamsin opened her wrist panel one-handed, steadying it against her thigh.

A queue of station notices filled the display: injury acknowledgement, lost-equipment assessment, rescue claim, berth-impact charge. The notices stacked in clean white boxes while the pod walls still ticked from cooling stress. The Junkyard could put a price beside anything before the air stopped shaking.

Tamsin dismissed all four with four firm movements of her thumb.

"I’ve got a ship," she said.

Kaelen glanced around the pod. His eyebrows lifted. "Then you should maintain your maneuvering pack."

"My ship."

"Apparently mine."

"Then consider me attached equipment."

She said it without gratitude and without asking. Her good shoulder stayed squared toward him. Kaelen had spent years keeping his inventory to things that did not need him back.

Tamsin watched him assess the offer as if it were damaged machinery. That was easier than thanks. Thanks could be refused, made smaller, or turned into debt.

A ship could not be shrugged away. It had weight even when it could not move.

He looked at her splinted arm, then at the dead fuel gauge.

The last cell was empty. His cutter was gone. The station would charge him for the bumper, the broken antenna, and perhaps the wheel handle if the worker at M-Seventeen had influence. Old arithmetic: one promising salvage claim had become a month of debt.

Blue Nine’s four hatch lights remained green.

Tamsin was breathing station air instead of the last oxygen in her suit.

Kaelen opened his account screen. The negative balance waited there in red, crisp and exact. His thumb rested over the figures for a moment before he closed it.

The numbers were accurate.

They were also missing the point.

His shoulders lowered by a small degree.

"What kind of ship?" he asked.

"The kind that needs work."

"All ships need work."

"This one has commitment."

"That is not a measurement."

"It becomes one when you see the hull."

Her panel requested a recipient identity. She entered his before he could object. Kaelen leaned forward, but she turned the display away from his reaching hand. The transfer field populated with an old station registry seal, then asked for a living-hand confirmation.

Kaelen looked at her. A crease formed between his brows.

"If it’s valuable," he said, "you don’t give it away after six minutes."

"If it were valuable, I wouldn’t own it."

"That’s closer to a measurement."

"The rescue claim will take anything liquid. The ship isn’t liquid. It barely qualifies as solid."

"Why me?"

Tamsin glanced at the four green lights outside, then at the rescue clamp status, which still insisted the impossible catch had been routine. Her gaze returned to his face.

She had owned the ship while it sat useless in the keel and every repair remained a future expense. Kaelen had spent the only useful thing he possessed before he knew her name.

She needed him to take the burden.

For once, she wanted it to land on someone who might answer it by opening a tool case.

"You burned a full cell on someone you didn’t know," she said. "Put half that effort into the hull and it may fly again."

She pressed her thumb to the confirmation field.

The pod verified her identity, checked the station registry, and sounded a bright two-note confirmation.

The first note cleared every notice from the wrist panel. The second sent gold light racing through the pod, up the console, and over the cracked viewport. The clean notes seemed too cheerful for the battered cabin. Then the deed stamped itself across the glass in letters large enough to cover the station beyond.

VESSEL DEED ACCEPTED
OWNER: KAELEN VOSS
VESSEL: WAYWARD SUN
BERTH COORDINATES: KEEL INTERIOR, BAY K-0

The gold words held, bright and undeniable.

Kaelen read the coordinates twice.

*A ship.*

His mouth opened, then closed. He leaned closer until the panel’s pale light sharpened the tired lines beneath his eyes.

Bay K-0 was not a berth. Kaelen had heard the name—every keel worker had—but nobody used the cavity itself. It was the sealed construction cavity inside the station’s keel, closed before he came to the Junkyard, its outer doors welded under three generations of repair plates. Keel miners used the surrounding bays because the old framing damped spindle vibration. The cavity was dead space.

A ship inside meant someone had put it there before the welds, or someone had found another way in.

A ship. Hidden in K-0.

Of course.

Gifts were trouble. Gifts during structural failure were worse.

Tamsin watched his face. Her good hand settled over the splint as the first flicker of satisfaction left her expression.

"There is one complication."

Kaelen kept looking at the coordinates. "There will be more than one."

"The ship is in the keel."

"I can read."

"The spindle is above the keel."

Kaelen looked through the cracked viewport.

The sunlight had shifted. Spar Three’s broken root turned toward them, pulling a chain of wrecks across the station’s bright face. Each hulk tugged the next through stretched docking lines and folded gantries. The main spindle no longer looked straight. Its central section bowed under forces the display had not finished naming. Repair plates along the bend lifted at their edges, making a broken ridge of reflected light.

The proximity panel flared red.

The gold deed vanished beneath a violent wash of crimson. Every cabin light strobed once and died. A low emergency pulse struck through the deck, climbed Kaelen’s boots, and closed around his ribs. The pod hurled the warning across the viewport in three blows.

MAIN SPINDLE SHEAR IMMINENT
PROJECTED SWEEP: KEEL INTERIOR
TIME TO CONTACT: 00:08:42

The final line burned without blinking.

Its warning glow filled the pod, caught in the cracks across the viewport, and turned Tamsin’s silver-streaked cheek the color of rust.

Kaelen’s body went still. Tamsin pushed herself higher against the couch frame, the movement tightening her face around the pain in her shoulder.

Beyond the cracked viewport, the spindle bent another degree. Sunlight ran along its length, clear and brilliant, as it began to fall toward everything beneath it.
