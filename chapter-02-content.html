Through the narrow horizontal slit of the forward viewport, Kaelen saw the bay's outer launch doors—two massive slabs of cast iron, held together at the center by a thick, hand-welded steel seam.

"We don't open them!" Tamsin yelled, her eyes wild beneath her helmet liner. "We run through them!"

The *Wayward Sun* didn't have a helm.

It had a forward cargo pit—a three-meter-wide steel depression set two steps below the main deck, where the surveyor's heavy sensor gear was supposed to have been mounted. Right now, the pit held two iron chairs bolted to the deck plates, a raw mechanical throttle quadrant wired directly to the reactor fuel valves, and a pair of heavy foot-pedals that controlled the lateral thruster bypass valves.

Kaelen dropped into the left chair. He didn't bother looking for a harness; the belt had been cut away years ago. He jammed his heavy boots onto the lateral pedals, grabbed the iron T-bar throttle with both hands, and looked out through the narrow viewport slit.

Bay K-0 was disintegrating around them.

The main station spindle had finally sheared. Millions of tons of structural iron, heavy utility piping, and crushed habitat drums were plunging down through the keel like a glacier made of dark metal. The roof of the bay was gone entirely, replaced by a churning sky of falling debris that blotted out the distant stars.

A twelve-meter structural I-beam, spinning end over end, crashed straight through the bay's upper framing and smashed into the launch track twenty feet ahead of the ship's nose.

The impact tore the track from its floor mountings, twisting the heavy steel rail into a sharp, upward-curving lip that blocked the exit.

"Throttle up!" Tamsin barked, dropping into the chair beside him and wedging her splinted left arm firmly against the pedestal frame. "If we stay on these cradles when the spindle hits the bay floor, the keel's going to fold us like a tin can!"

Kaelen didn't answer. He couldn't.

The world had begun to narrow around his eyes.

The cold under his ribs—the cold he had been holding back since Shaft M-Seventeen—was no longer a swallowed stone. It was a flood. It rose into his chest, rushed down the nerves of his right arm, and poured into his palm before he could close his fingers against it.

His right arm went numb to the elbow.

"Voss!" Tamsin shouted, her voice cutting through the growing roar of the collapsing roof. "The throttle! Hit it now or we're buried!"

He heard her through a wall of ice. He wasn't ignoring her; he was just trying not to let his diaphragm freeze solid.

Space around the ship didn't look empty anymore.

The dark paths he had seen in Ch.1—the fine, measurement-crowded future-lines—didn't appear across the viewport glass. Something heavier, thicker took their place.

The air inside the bay looked dense, bent, distorted by the massive iron shapes plunging through the ceiling. Kaelen could feel the mass of the falling spindle—not as a sight, but as a crushing, physical weight pressing against his forehead. He could feel the way the heavy structural beam had bent the space around itself, creating a trough of steep, unnatural curvature that was drawing loose scrap toward its path like water sliding down an iron funnel.

He wasn't seeing futures. He was touching the shape of the room.

Too heavy to dodge. He couldn't dodge a falling mountain with an uncalibrated thruster—he had to convince the mountain it belonged somewhere else.

*One variable.*

His right hand tightened around the cold iron throttle handle. The skin across his forearm turned a pale, marble gray, the veins standing out in dark blue lines beneath the surface.

He didn't pull a future toward him. He reached into the space three meters ahead of the ship's nose—where the first wave of falling girders and shattered armor plate was descending—and twisted local metric tension.

He created a gravity shadow.

The Gravity Shadow

"Voss, your arm!" Tamsin gasped, her good hand reaching out toward his shoulder before stopping mid-air. "What's happening to your skin?"

"Don't touch me," he forced out through gritted teeth. "Just brace!"

It was an alcove carved out of local curvature—a temporary, steep fold in the local geometry that made mass want to move anywhere except through the three-meter envelope directly in front of the *Wayward Sun*.

The cold went deeper than it had all day.

It passed his elbow, bit into his shoulder socket, and locked his jaw so hard his back teeth clicked together. A harsh, black lattice unfolded across his vision—not fine measurement lines, but a heavy, geometric web that branched from every falling scrap-heap in the bay. For half a second, his sight split wide: he saw the ship sitting still on her cradles, smashed flat by a ten-ton iron plate, and he saw her moving through an alcove of bent space while the plate slid harmlessly overhead.

The shadow flickered.

A sharp, stabbing pain slammed into the back of his skull behind his right eye, blinding him with a sudden burst of white light.

*Hold it.*

Kaelen slammed the iron T-bar throttle forward with his left hand.

The *Wayward Sun* screamed.

Her three broad copper thruster bells ignited all at once with a sound that tore through the ship's iron ribs like a physical blow. The pulse-arc reactor roared, dumping raw, hot plasma into the exhaust manifold. The ship lunged off her mounting cradles with a violence that threw Kaelen forward against the T-bar, his chin striking the iron grip hard enough to taste copper instantly.

The ship shot down the launch track toward the twisted steel lip.

Above her nose, a ten-ton slab of station armor was falling straight for the viewport.

Tamsin let out a sharp, choked breath, her right hand reaching out instinctively to brace against the front dash—

The armor plate hit the gravity shadow.

It didn't smash the ship. It didn't touch the glass.

The ten-ton slab seemed to hesitate in mid-air three meters before the bow, its leading edge tilting unnaturally sideways as if sliding down an invisible glass hill. It veered violently to the left, caught the air, and crashed into the bay wall with a deafening roar, leaving the passage ahead clear.

"What..." Tamsin's voice was barely a whisper over the roar of the engines. "What did you just do?"

Kaelen couldn't speak. Black spots were dancing across his eyes, threatening to swallow the narrow viewport slit entirely. The cold in his arm had turned into a burning, icy ache that ran from his knuckles straight to his armpit.

The ship was twenty meters from the launch doors.

The twisted rail-lip was right ahead—a jagged steel wedge that would catch the ship's lower hull and tear her belly open before she could reach the doors.

"The rail's buckled!" Tamsin yelled, bracing her hip hard against the pedestal frame. "We're going to catch the nose!"

"We're riding it," Kaelen forced through his teeth. Ride it or die on it—it came down to the same difference.

He needed something smaller. Something simple.

The clamp-catch trick from Ch.1.

He reached out with his mind—not to the space around the ship, but to the launch sled shoes mounted under the ship's forward keel. He found the short-horizon local path of the shoe, found the position where the metal *would* meet the rail if the track weren't bent, and nudged the shoe's alignment two centimeters to the left.

One size up. Sled shoe to rail track.

*Catch—*

The right forward shoe struck the bent rail-lip, but instead of digging in and tearing the hull, it caught the nudged angle, rode up the steel curve like a ramp, and launched the thirty-ton surveyor ship straight off the track.

The *Wayward Sun* went airborne inside the bay.

"Hold your teeth!" Tamsin barked as negative-G snatched them upward.

Her heavy alloy nose slammed straight into the center seam of the outer launch doors.

Breaking the Launch Doors

The cast-iron doors—welded forty years ago by riggers who had dreamed of open air—didn't hold. The center weld snapped with a sound like a thunderclap. The heavy iron slabs folded outward into space, shedding red tamper-seal, old rivets, and shattered mounting frames in a brilliant silver fan.

The ship punched through the gap into vacuum.

Behind her, Bay K-0, the freight rail shaft, and the entire lower keel of the Junkyard folded in on themselves as the main spindle finally arrived, crushing the construction cavity into a flat, lifeless ribbon of twisted iron.

The *Wayward Sun* cleared the wreck, her three copper bells spitting raw golden fire into the dark, and flew out into open space.

Silence hit them like a wall.

In vacuum, the deafening roar of the dying station vanished instantly, replaced by the thin, rapid rasp of Kaelen's breath inside his suit and the wet, heavy thrumming of the pulse-arc reactor under the floor plates.

The forward viewport showed open space.

It was vast, black, and completely indifferent to the station that had just died behind them.

"Did we clear it?" Tamsin asked, her voice raspy and thin over the suit communicator.

"We're in the clear," Kaelen said, his throat raw. "We've got vacuum behind us and nothing in front."

Kaelen slumped back into the left cargo chair. His right arm lay uselessly across his lap, the gray receding from his skin in a slow, dark flush of returning blood. Under his ribs, the cold had settled into a thin line of ache that traced from his wrist to his elbow. It didn't have a name. He didn't give it one.

His fingers twitched when he willed them to move. Small victories.

Beside him, Tamsin Vale slowly let go of the forward dash.

Her face was white, her forehead dripping with sweat that ran down into her short hair. She looked through the narrow viewport slit, her eyes wide, watching the Junkyard drift away behind them.

The station was no longer a ring. Spar Three drifted two kilometers to the south, a trailing tail of frozen atmosphere marking its path. The central spindle had broken into three sections, tumbling through a cloud of glittering scrap and shattered solar mirrors. Halo Two was a twisted C-shape, its outer hull scorched black where three pressure trunks had vented to space.

Five years of shifts, Kaelen thought, watching a drifting cluster of yellow insulation foam sweep across the viewport. Reduced to salvage before the shift whistle even blew.

On the open radio band, the chaos had begun to thin into the cold, organized brevity of disaster recovery:

"—Tug Four to any available vessel... we have thirty-two survivors from Blue Nine on board... life support is at ninety percent... requesting vector to the Outer Boundary Station—"

"—this is Rigger Sled Twelve... Spar Three is silent... no beacon responses from the upper shops—"

"—Halo Control, do you copy? Halo Control?"

No one answered for Halo Control. The station's main transmission tower was tumbling end over end three hundred meters below the *Wayward Sun*'s port flank, its long red antenna snapped off at the root.

Kaelen reached out with his left hand and clicked the radio receiver off.

The cabin went quiet again.

"They're away," Tamsin said softly, her voice barely carrying over the reactor's pulse. She was looking at the small cluster of work tugs and rescue sleds that were pulling clear of the wreckage, their small blue thruster flares moving slowly toward the deep-space shipping lanes. "Blue Nine made it out."

"Some of them," Kaelen said. His voice sounded like dry gravel. "Not all."

He looked down at his right hand. The red net under his skin was gone, but the flesh was tender, like a fresh burn. His account screen was gone too—destroyed with Pod Twelve and the station's berth computers. The eleven weeks of fees, the replacement membrane cost, the four debts he had been measuring his life against—all of them had died with the station's ledger.

He was twenty-four years old, sitting in a broken surveyor ship with a dead life-support loop, an empty stomach, and a hand that couldn't hold a tool without shaking.

"Voss," Tamsin said.

He turned his head slowly.

She was leaning back against her chair, her good right hand resting on the iron throttle quadrant. She looked battered, grimy, her splinted left arm held tightly against her ribs, but her eyes were steady.

"We have no nav computer," she said, listing the facts without emotion. "We have three suit tanks of oxygen left between us. The starboard thruster manifold is leaking plasma, and the main hull needs forty hours of dress-welding before we can hit thirty percent light-speed."

She paused, her mouth pulling at one corner in a dry, tired half-smile.

"You're the owner," she said.

The line sat between them in the quiet cabin.

Kaelen looked at her—at the silver sealant smeared across her cheek, at the Guild badge still tucked into her sleeve, at the steady, unyielding set of her shoulders.

"I'm the one who knows where every seam is," she added.

Kaelen's lips moved into the ghost of a deadpan smile. The ache behind his right eye was still pulsing, but the world had stopped doubling. Space outside the viewport stayed where it was.

"Then you know which side to stay on," Kaelen said.

Tamsin let out a single, rough bark of a laugh that ended in a short wince as her bad shoulder objected. "I'm on the side that isn't falling, salvager. That's close enough for today."

Neither of them said anything about working alone. The phrase had died somewhere in the dark between Bay K-0 and the launch doors, leaving behind a quiet, unspoken truth: two people who knew how to use a wrench were harder to kill than one.

Kaelen reached out with his left hand, engaged the manual thruster lock, and leveled the *Wayward Sun*'s nose toward the outer rim of the sector.

Ahead of them lay the boundary—the cold, open dark where the human domains ended and the deeper, untamed reach of the Lattice began. There were no station berths out there, no Guild inspectors, no berth offices demanding fees for the privilege of breathing.

There was only a broken ship that needed work.

And Kaelen Voss had a tool roll.

The *Wayward Sun* flexed her iron ribs, her three copper bells burning golden against the black, and pointed her bow into the clear air.

Into the Clear Air
